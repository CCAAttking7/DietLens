System Analysis and Design Group Project — Assignment 2 Part II: Design  |  Weight: 30%  |  2026-2027, 1st Semester For Part II of the project, you should turn your Part I requirements into a system design, covering the logical, deployment, and technology architecture, supported by visual design models (such as C4, UML, and SysML diagrams) and the key design decisions with their rationale. As in real practice, you may refine your requirements as the design exposes gaps or conflicts; note what changed and why. If your system is agentic, the design should also address its special concerns, such as agent roles, tool interfaces, memory and state, orchestration, and guardrails.  The quality of your design decisions matters more than the volume of your documentation. You are encouraged to use other graphical or visual representations that convey your design more efficiently. Present your progress in class (Week 9 or Week 10) and then submit your final design artifacts by the end of Week 10, incorporating the feedback you received. 1. Purpose and Design Mindset  Approach this assignment as a software designer and architect, not simply as someone translating requirements into components. Adopt a problem driven mindset: explore the business domain to understand real needs and constraints, and investigate the technology landscape to understand capabilities, limitations, and trade-offs. Good design begins with understanding the problem, not choosing a solution. Think beyond individual components to the system as a whole, evaluate alternatives against the system’s goals, and justify not only what you choose, but why. Your design should provide an architectural blueprint for implementation, supported by detailed models of critical parts and explicit rationale for key design decisions. Make the overall structure coherent while examining complex or consequential parts in greater depth. In the next assignment, Part III, you will use agentic development to build prototypes and proofs of concept (PoCs), test your design decisions, uncover architectural limitations, and iteratively refine your design. Aim for a design that is concrete enough to guide implementation and explicit about its assumptions, trade-offs, and potential weaknesses. 2. Notes on Architecture and key decisions Demonstrate systems-level thinking across three architectural views: • Logical architecture: Major subsystems or components, responsibilities, and relationships. • Deployment architecture: Runtime environments, deployment nodes, components distribution. • Technology architecture: Selected languages, frameworks, platforms, services, and models, including alternatives considered • Key decisions: Alternatives and rationale, documented in a decision log or ADRs (Architecture Decision Records). • Quality attributes: Priorities such as performance, security, and maintainability, and how the architecture addresses them.

• Architectural risks: Key risks and mitigation strategies. • System level diagram: At least one architecture diagram (C4, UML, SysML, or another visual form), accompanied by a narrative. • Agentic systems: Address relevant agent specific design concerns. An agile solution architect does not decide everything upfront. Identify the decisions that matter most, justify them carefully, and leave room for less critical choices to evolve. B. Notes on Design models Understand the problem before committing to solutions. Model use case realizations where appropriate: • Class diagrams: Key abstractions, responsibilities, and important attributes. • Interaction diagrams: Sequence or collaboration diagrams showing how components work together to realize important use cases. • Notation: C4, UML, SysML, or any graphical or visual representation that communicates and specifies the design effectively. 4. Encouraged Practices The following practices are encouraged: • Reflection: Revisit Part I assumptions and requirements, explaining what changed and why. • Continuous architecting: Evolve the architecture as your understanding develops. • Design thinking: Reframe the problem when underlying assumptions prove questionable. • Design for validation: Identify critical decisions that a prototype or PoC can test. • Agentic patterns: Where applicable, consider planner and executor separation, tool use boundaries, human oversight, and appropriate levels of agent autonomy.
